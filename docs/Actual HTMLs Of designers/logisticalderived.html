Pulsar4X · component designer · door 4 of the remaining eight · updated 2026-07-30 — colonists

Thirteen templates, and every one of them is the same two attributes with a different label on the box.

Cargo holds, warehouses, fuel tanks, ordnance racks, magazines, troop bays, spaceports, shuttlebays. Read what the simulation actually receives and there are exactly two things: a store (what cargo type, how much volume) and a mover (how fast, how far). Everything else is the same pair with a different string. So the door is one question with two answers, one choice, and two sliders — and it reproduces all thirteen.

🔴 And it is by far the worst-maintained door in the designer. Seven findings, and three of them are things that are actively wrong in the shipped data rather than merely un-dialled: a template that is defined twice and silently merged, a fuel tank that asks for sixty-five billion crew, and a storage formula that subtracts a rate from a volume and comes out negative.

🔑 It is also where the Chassis door's dead dial turns out to have a home. A ground frame's Size — documented, unread, free — is exactly the number the transport system should consume, and instead the transport reads a hard-coded three-value table. Two doors, one missing wire.

The whole door, as two questions

What the simulation receives from a logistical component. That is the entire input surface — two attributes.

Does it HOLD something?

Cargo typeVolume

CargoStorageAtb(storeTypeID, maxVolume). Two fields. The type is a string — general-storage, fuel-storage, ordnance-storage — and the volume is added to the host's store. Nine of the thirteen templates are this and nothing else.

Does it MOVE something?

Transfer rateTransfer range

CargoTransferAtb(rate, range). Two fields. How fast cargo crosses, and from how far away. Four templates are this, and two of them are both at once.

The three special stores

Ammo, in kgTroops, in carry-sizeFuel, in m³

They look like separate systems and they are not — they are cargo types with their own consumer. Ammo feeds the combat resolver's dry-magazine gate; troops feed the invasion chain; fuel feeds the engine. Same "a store of type X, volume V" shape.

What is NOT here

Logistic Capacity

LogiBaseAtb — a real attribute, on a real template, with a real 5–100 dial, and zero readers anywhere in the codebase outside its own file. The only completely dead attribute found in eight doors.

🔑 So the collapse is the same shape as Weapons'. Thirteen templates × up to four dials each → one choice (what it holds) and two sliders (how much · and if it moves, rate ↔ range). Nothing is lost: a magazine is a store of ammo, a troop bay is a store of troops, a fuel tank is a store of fuel, a spaceport is a store that also moves. The differences that matter are which consumer reads the store — and that already falls out of the cargo type.

🔴 spaceport is defined twice, and one of them is silently eaten

Two different templates, in two different files, with the same UniqueID. The mod loader does not skip the second one and does not warn about it — it merges it property-by-property over the first, and the load order decides who wins.

🔴 The consequence: the spaceport a colony builds stores nothing. ModLoader.ApplyModGeneric takes the Default operation branch on a duplicate ID and reflects over every non-null property, replacing it — including the whole Properties list, because no CollectionOperation is specified. So Warehouse Size, Total Cargo Stored and the DBargsStorage attribute are gone. default-design-spaceport is a shipped design, unlocked on Earth, Kithrin, the UMF and the devtest faction — so every start in the game has a spaceport that can only move cargo, never hold it.
⚠ And the existing guard cannot see it. BaseModIntegrityTests.BaseMod_LoadsWithNoSkippedEntries was written for exactly this class of bug — a blueprint silently dropped at load. But a merge is not a skip: nothing is added to SkippedEntries, no console line is printed, the template count is unchanged, and the resulting blueprint is valid. It is invisible to every gauge in the project.

🔑 And it is not the only one. Swept every UniqueID across all twenty template files: 300 ids, seven collisions. spaceport (storage vs installations) · stainless-steel (particleMaterials vs materials) · water (particleMaterials vs minerals) · ground-balanced · ground-offensive · ground-defensive (groundStances vs combatDoctrines) · and hydrogen-sulphide, twice inside the same file. Some may be deliberate overrides. None of them says so, and none of them is checked.

✅ The cheap gauge: a test that loads the base mod and asserts no UniqueID appears in two template files unless it is on an explicit allow-list. It is a dozen lines, it runs in milliseconds, and it closes a whole class of silent data bug that no existing test can see. That is the first thing I would build in this door — before any dial work, because a merged template makes every other measurement here unreliable.

✅ Look at what the good one does, because it is the model for this whole door. You set the volume you want. It solves the radius from the volume, then computes the mass of the steel shell needed to contain it: 1.333π(r³ − (r−0.004)³) × 8000. A 2,500 m³ tank comes out at 8.42 m radius and 28.5 tonnes dry. That is a real number, derived from a real wall thickness, and it means a bigger tank costs more steel for a physical reason — the square-cube law again, arrived at honestly. Nothing in this door needs designing; it needs the good template copied over the bad one.
🔴 Same class of bug as the reactor's CrewReq: "[Mass]", and worse by seven orders of magnitude. Crew is a real scarce pool (ManpowerTools.ResolveBuild) and component crew sums into a ship's requirement and a colony's infrastructure demand, so a fuel-cargo-hold is not merely mis-priced — it can never be built by anyone. It ships unlocked with no design, which is the only reason nobody has hit it. The fix is to delete the template, not to re-tune it: its honest twin already ships four working designs.

🔴 And a third crew bug in the same door: space-port (the hyphenated one — yes, there are three spaceports) has CrewReq: "1000000", hard-coded, on a 500-tonne facility. One million people to run a cargo dock. It is not unlocked anywhere and has no design, so it is latent — but it is the third template in one door whose crew figure makes it unbuildable, which stops being a coincidence and starts being a pattern: nobody has ever costed this door.

🔴 A storage formula that subtracts a rate from a volume — and comes out negative

The ordnance rack and the (eaten) planetary spaceport share this line:

Total Cargo Stored = Rack Size − Size Efficiency − Cargo Transfer Rate
Rack Size is a volume in m³. Cargo Transfer Rate is a rate, in units per second. Subtracting one from the other is not a balance decision; it is a units error.

🔴 And nothing clamps it. CargoStorageAtb.OnComponentInstallation does db.TypeStores[type].MaxVolume += MaxVolume — raw, unguarded, no floor at zero. So installing a default-configured ordnance rack subtracts 500 m³ from the ship's existing ordnance store. The shipped design escapes only because somebody happened to set the transfer rate to 100 while tuning it to hit 2.5 t. Every design a player makes at the template defaults is broken, and it fails quietly by making the ship's magazine smaller.

✅ The fix is two characters and a clamp. Transfer rate should not be subtracted from storage at all — a dock's throughput does not consume its warehouse. If the intent was "the machinery takes up room", that is what Size Efficiency already does. And CargoStorageAtb should floor MaxVolume at zero on principle, the way every other attribute in the codebase clamps its constructor arguments.

⚠️ Rate vs Range — a split dial that destroys value in both directions

This one looks exactly right. It is a single 1–9 dial that trades transfer rate against transfer range, which is the honest split-dial shape the whole campaign has been asking for. Run the arithmetic across its span and it fails the ladder test — backwards.

🔴 The product is not invariant — it collapses by 5.2× while the rate you are buying rises only 1.73×. Rate is Size×0.5 + Size×0.5×RvR×0.1 (additive, so it saturates) while range is Size×0.5 − Size×0.5×RvR×0.1 (subtractive, so it goes to zero). You give up 89% of your range to gain 73% more rate. By §34.7a that makes 1 strictly dominant for anyone who cares about range at all — and the fact that the shipped shuttlebay sits at 2 is why nobody noticed: the one design in the game is already parked at the good end.
✅ And the fix keeps the dial, which matters — it is the right idea. Make it multiplicative on both sides, the way the RTG's output-versus-lifetime split already is: rate = base × k and range = base ÷ k. Then rate × range is flat by construction, a dock genuinely chooses between reaching further and moving faster, and neither end is free. The template already has the correct SHAPE; it just has a plus where it needs a multiply.

Build one

One question with two answers, one choice, two sliders — and it reproduces all thirteen templates plus the two new people-compartments. Every control below says what it changes and which number it writes. Each answer opens on its real shipped part, so the panel starts by reproducing the game rather than a guess.

① The door — does it hold, or does it move?

This is the forced choice: it decides which attribute the component gets, and therefore which sliders exist below. Hold → CargoStorageAtb. Move → CargoTransferAtb. Both → both, on one component.

② What does it hold?

Writes one string — the CargoTypeID — into CargoStorageAtb. That single string is the only real difference between nine of the thirteen templates. It also decides which consumer in the engine can read the contents, shown in the readout as Its consumer.

②b How are they carried — awake, or frozen?

A second forced choice, because it swaps the CargoTypeID and the room one person needs: a berth is 10 m³, a cryo pod is 2 m³ (PassengerPacking.cs). Five times the people in the same hull — which is why, as built, cryo has no downside and strictly wins. Tick the fixes box to give it one.

🔑 The Chassis door's dead dial has a home, and it is here

A ground frame carries a Size dial: 1–10 on a human frame, 1–60 on a tracked chassis. Its own source says it "feeds transport carry-size (a bigger frame → more bay room)." Grepped the tree in the Chassis pass: zero readers. Here is what reads carry-size instead:

GroundTransport.CarrySizeOf(GroundUnitType type) => type switch { Infantry => 1.0, Artillery => 2.0, Armor => 3.0, _ => 1.0 };
A hard-coded three-value table on the unit's type enum — not on the design.

🔑 So one troop bay hauls a thousand Titans, and the designer's whole "anyone can build anything" promise breaks at the loading ramp. The atb's own documentation describes the correct behaviour — "how MANY fit is a size calc (bay capacity vs each unit's carry-size), not a fixed slot count" — and the size calc reads a table that predates the frame dial. Both halves were built; nobody connected them.

✅ The fix is one line, and it makes two doors' dead ends into one working mechanic. CarrySizeOf reads the unit's frame Size when it has one, falling back to the type table when it does not (a code-built or modded unit). The Chassis door's free dial starts costing bay room; the Logistical door's bay capacity starts meaning something; and a Titan stops fitting in a rifleman's seat. This is what the Prime Directive means by "a system is only as done as its connections."

What this kills

🔴 LogiBaseAtb — the only completely dead attribute in eight doors

A real attribute class, a real template (logistics-office), a real dial (Logistic Capacity, 5–100), a real shipped design (default-design-logistics-office). And every single reference to it in the entire codebase is inside its own file — the class declaration, its own install hook, its own constructor. Nothing reads what it writes. It is not even unlocked on Earth, so no player has ever built one. The logistics system it was meant to gate (Logistics/, the automated cargo routes) does not consult it. Either wire it as the cap on how many logistics routes a colony can run — which is a genuinely good mechanic and the obvious intent — or delete it. What it must not stay is a dial that looks live.

🔴 troop-bay — Mass is the hard-coded constant 5000

So Capacity (1 → 60) and CarryClass (personnel vs vehicle) are both free: a sixty-troop bay weighs the same five tonnes as a one-troop bay, takes the same ten crew, and costs the same materials. The same constant-mass leak the Chassis door found on three of its four chassis — which makes it a pattern worth naming: a hard-coded Mass makes every dial on that template free at a stroke, because all seven cost channels are computed from [Mass].

⚠️ warehouse-facility — the shipped design asks for 100× what the template allows

Storage Volume is capped at 10,000 by the template's MaxFormula, and default-design-warehouse sets it to 1,000,000. It clamps silently. This one is already known and already cost the project a day — it is the storage cap that jammed the whole colony economy and quarantined EconomyReadoutTests for a week (the fix was to drop the start stockpiles, not to raise the cap). Recorded here because it is the same door and the same failure mode: a design asking for something the template will not give it, with no error.

✅ ship-magazine — the one honest cost CHAIN in the door, and the only one with research

Mass = Ammo Capacity × 1.2. Volume, crew, credits, build points and materials all follow from it, so there is no way to buy capacity cheaply. And it is the only logistical template in the game with a non-zero ResearchCost — every other one is "0", so a magazine is the one piece of logistics you have to discover before you can build it. Copy this cost structure onto the other twelve and most of this door's problems are gone.

⚠ One caveat, so this is not oversold: its crew coefficient is [Mass] × 0.02, which puts 120 people on the shipped 5,000 kg magazine. That is a calibration question, not a structural one — the chain is right, the constant is steep — but it is worth a look while you are in there, because crew is now a scarce pool.

🔴 A cargo hold weighs one percent of what it holds — so the mass budget cannot constrain cargo

Mass = Size Efficiency × 1.0, and Size Efficiency = Max(1, Storage Volume × 0.01). So a 1,000 m³ cargo hold weighs 10 kg, and the 10,000 m³ ceiling weighs 100 kg. The shipped "Cargo Hold 5t" masses 50 kg. Its own Volume is computed correctly (volume + machinery, so it takes up real room) — only the mass is off by two orders of magnitude.

🔑 Which is a Chassis-door problem wearing a Logistical hat. A ship's hull enforces a mass budget, and ShipDesign sums MassPerUnit against it — so cargo capacity is very nearly free against the one wall a ship has. Even with the Chassis budget properly priced, a freighter could carry an unbounded hold. The two findings compound: fix the budget and cargo still escapes it. A hold's mass should track the tankage that contains it, exactly the way the good fuel tank already does — a steel shell around a volume, which is a real number with a real square-cube law in it.

Where the door stands

One question — does it hold something, or move something? Two answers plus "both", one choice (what it holds), two sliders (how much · rate ↔ range). It reproduces all thirteen templates and collapses ~30 dials to one choice and two sliders.

🔴 The headline is that this door is not under-dialled, it is UNMAINTAINED. Three of the seven findings are live data defects rather than missing decisions: a template defined twice and silently merged so every start's spaceport stores nothing · a fuel tank asking sixty-five billion crew · and a storage formula that subtracts a rate from a volume and goes negative at the template defaults. The other four are the familiar shape: a dead attribute, a constant mass making every dial free, a split dial that loses value, and a known clamp.

🔑 And it compounds with Chassis in both directions. A cargo hold weighs 1% of what it holds (a 1,000 m³ hold masses 10 kg), and a ship's only wall is a mass budget — so even a correctly-priced hull cannot constrain cargo. The good fuel tank shows the fix: mass is the steel shell around the volume, which brings its own square-cube law.

🔑 And it holds the campaign's clearest cross-door wire. The Chassis door's dead Size dial is exactly what GroundTransport.CarrySizeOf should read, and instead it reads a hard-coded three-value table — so a Titan takes the same bay room as a rifleman. One line connects two doors' dead ends into a working mechanic.

✅ Two things here are genuinely excellent and should be copied, not changed: stainless-steel-fuel-tank, which derives its mass from the geometry of a 4 mm steel shell, and ship-magazine, the only logistical template with an honest cost chain and the only one that costs research at all.

The order I would build it in, and the first slice is not a dial: L1 — the duplicate-UniqueID test (a dozen lines; a merged template makes every other measurement in this door untrustworthy, and no existing gauge can see it). Then L2 the negative-storage fix + a clamp on CargoStorageAtb, L3 the flat rate × range product, L4 delete fuel-cargo-hold, L5 the CarrySizeOf wire, L6 mass for the troop bay. Your ruling is needed on exactly one thing: LogiBaseAtb — wire it as the cap on a colony's logistics routes, or delete it? It is the only genuinely dead attribute in the designer, and it has been dead long enough that either answer is defensible.

⑪ THE DOOR ITSELF — "what is it carrying?"

Your ruling: "for cargo, you should also include things like food, people (some general term), resources both refined and unrefined, and also ships/other entities, anything else that would be shipped, stored, or needs to be moved."

The mechanism first, because it decides everything else. Anything carryable declares a CargoTypeID — the KIND of compartment it needs. A hold declares the same string through CargoStorageAtb(storeTypeID, maxVolume). CargoMath.GetFreeVolume looks the item's string up in the hold's compartment list — and on a miss it returns 0. No exception, no log. So "this ship physically cannot carry that" and "this ship has no room right now" are the same reading on the same gauge. That is why everything below survived unnoticed.

🔒 The rule that keeps the taxonomy honest

A cargo class exists only if it needs a physically DIFFERENT compartment. If two things can ride in the same box, they are the same class.

That is what stops this becoming thirty flavours of crate — and it is the §39.8 rule again: each class must win one axis outright. The axes are the requirements a box has to meet: pressure · temperature · continuous power · containment · an acceleration limit · what failure costs · and whether the contents pour or take a seat.

⑬ THE DOCK — moved OUT of this door and built as its own area

Your ruling, and it corrects the table above: "did you build berth for an actual dock that can be built? that should be its own area." Berth should never have been in the cargo-class list.

Every compartment measures its contents in cubic metres poured in. A dock holds a discrete vessel that arrives and leaves under its own power, so the question it answers is how many, and how big — not how much fits. Pouring a frigate into a warehouse by volume would let you carry half a frigate. That is exactly why GroundBayAtb had to invent its own capacity system for troops instead of using a cargo type.

⑭ COLONISTS — the cargo with no consumer, and the reason you cannot colonise

The taxonomy pass listed colonists as item three of five: a good that still needed a consumer. That was the wrong reading. Tracing what actually consumes them turned up something much larger, and it is not a small bug — it is a missing verb in the core loop.

🔴 The finding

Population in this engine is multiplicative-only. Every write to a colony's population is a multiply, and the setter is a set, never an add:

newPop = (long)(value * (1.0 + growthRate + migration - starvation));  // PopulationProcessor
population[id] = newPopulation;  // UpdatePopulation — a SET

There is no pop + n anywhere in the codebase. Population can only ever scale what is already there — like a pump with no prime, it can run forever and never move anything. So zero is an absorbing state.

And zero is exactly where colonies start:

EvidenceWhat it says
ColonyFactory.CreateColony(faction, species, planet, long initialPopulation = 0)the default is zero
CreateColonyOrder.Execute (:52)calls it with three arguments — so it takes that default
CreateColonyOrder.IsValidCommandreturns true unconditionally — no ship, no cost, no people, no distance
ExpandResolver.cs:23 — the AI's own comment"an instant order (no colony ship, no landing): CreateColonyOrder → ColonyFactory"
DefaultStartFactory.cs:198🔴 founds Mars with no population argument — it ships at zero and stays there for the whole campaign
Founding works. Populating does not exist. You can plant a colony anywhere, instantly and for free, and it will be an empty shell forever. The second X of 4X has no mechanism.

🔑 And "migration" is not movement either. MigrationRate(morale) is a ± fraction applied to a colony's own population — morale above 50 makes that colony grow, below 50 makes it shrink. Nobody ever leaves one place and arrives at another. It is a birth-and-death modifier wearing a movement name.

Why this reframes colonists from a nice-to-have into the keystone

On the taxonomy list, colonists were a good in search of a consumer. They are not. They are the only additive population source the game could have — the prime on the pump. Without one, "settle a new world" is a button that produces an empty shell, and docs/MVP.md's finish line — "you can take a planet" — is hollow, because you cannot then hold it with people.

The design — derived, not invented

✅ The precedent already exists, and it settles the hard question. TeamObject : ICargoable (Tech/TeamObject.cs:9) is a research team you can already load onto a ship: CargoTypeID = passenger-storage, MassPerUnit = teamSize × 100 kg, volume from PassengerPacking. People are already a carryable cargo type in this engine.

That matters because of the intrinsic test (§1a). A colonist is not generic — a human colonist is not a Kithrin one — so the good must carry a species. A JSON ProcessedMaterial has no species field, and authoring one material per species is a combinatorial mess that grows every time a species is added. But TeamObject proves the engine already supports a runtime, per-instance cargo object carrying its own payload. A ColonistPacket holding SpeciesId + Count is that same shape, already load-bearing.
