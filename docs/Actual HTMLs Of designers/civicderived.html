The biggest door by dial count and the most entangled, because the morale plan already built half of it. So the job here was never to design morale - it was to check whether the model that exists is actually being fed.

And one input is not fed at all. ColonyMoraleDB has a complete, two-sided, documented employment term: up to +15 morale for full employment, down to -25 for unemployment - a forty-point band, the widest single factor in the model. It reads GetTotalJobs, which sums EmploymentAtbDB.Jobs across a colony's installed components. No template in the game carries EmploymentAtbDB. So jobs is always 0, the ratio is always the "no data" sentinel, and employment contributes exactly zero, forever.

And the producer already exists as data - it is just not published. A factory's CrewReq is Size x 5. That number is how many people the factory employs. Every installation in the game already declares its headcount; none of them declares it as a job. The fix is to publish what is already computed.

This door also holds the single best-priced template in the whole game - and it correctly implements the exact trade the Enhancers door gives away for free.

And the fix needs no new design and almost no new data, because the number already exists. Every installation already declares a CrewReq - a factory needs Size x 0.5 people (recalibrated from x5), a refinery [Mass] x 0.1, a mine Area x 0.0005 (from x0.005), a research lab 20. Those figures ARE the jobs the installation provides. One derivation, and a forty-point morale band comes alive on every colony in the game - including the deliberate failure case where you build industry faster than population.
CREW CALIBRATION — the Civic half (paired with the Industrial door's mine/factory 10× cut). Publishing CrewReq as jobs exposes a structural hole: in the shipped set only mines and factories carry real labour — infrastructure is a flat 10 — so once the two steep industry coeffs are cut, a colony employs almost nobody. The sector that actually staffs a country is services, and it rides on population. Infrastructure now carries service employment: Crew = Support Colonists × k, and k is a DENSITY dial — it IS the employment half of the rural-vs-metropolis difference. A thin frontier town runs k ≈ 0.2 (1 service worker per 5 residents); a dense city runs k ≈ 0.5 — nearly its whole working-age population (½ of pop) employed in utilities, transit, healthcare, education, admin, retail and finance. Note the STRUCTURE: because infrastructure both houses and employs, the count of arcologies can't move the employment ratio (each adds pop AND jobs in fixed proportion) — only k can, which is why "fewer housing blocks" never reaches full employment and the service coefficient is the real lever. It also prices the once-free Support Colonists dial in labour, closing the constant-Mass gap. Downstream, docs/ground/planetview.html runs the super-city at k = 0.5 → ~90% employment, services the overwhelming employer — as in a real metropolis.

Which means it is also the biggest live behaviour change in any of the eight doors, and it should not be slipped in quietly. Turning employment on will move every colony's morale, in both directions, and morale feeds migration, tax income and legitimacy. Flag-gated and measured against the existing MoraleTests baseline - the same treatment the fuel gate got.

The best-priced template in the game - and it already fixes another door's leak

One template in eight doors prices three dials, one of them cubically, and gets a cross-cutting trade right that a different door gives away free. It is the farm.

CrewReq = Max(1, Food Output x 0.02 x (1 - Automation))

The cubic is the interesting one. Tripling food quality costs twenty-seven times the quality component of the mass - a deliberate, steep diminishing return on a dial whose benefit (a morale bonus above "not starving") is bounded. That is the ladder test not merely passed but passed aggressively: the top of the dial is reachable and expensive, which is exactly what you want from a luxury stat. Nobody in eight doors else wrote a cubic cost curve.

And look at the crew formula, because it is the Enhancers door's leak already solved. Automation reduces CrewReq multiplicatively and costs mass to do it - trading a scarce pool for a plentiful one, at a stated rate. That is precisely what the Enhancers door's crew-automation suite promises in its own description ("spend mass and high tech instead of scarce workforce") and then hands over free, because its Crew Reduction dial is not in its mass formula. Two templates, one mechanic, and the farm gets it right. That is the fourth time in this campaign the fix has turned out to be already written somewhere in the repository.

The one still unpriced: the Space Habitat. Its Mass = 1000 is a constant, so a station that houses a MILLION people costs one tonne, zero credits and a hundred build points - the same as one housing nobody. Population is the resource the entire colony game is built on - industry, research, tax, manpower and morale - and this ceiling is a free dial with a six-figure range. Life Support and Residency are now priced (mass tracks their dials); the habitat is the last constant-mass template in the door.
One thing here is genuinely well handled and worth defending: the habitat deliberately carries no gravity or pressure tolerance attribute at all, which is how a space station escapes a planet's environment. That is the right modelling choice, and it is also a documented landmine - you cannot achieve it by setting the tolerance dials to zero, because template property values are clamped to their tech-formula bounds at instantiation, so a 0 silently will not stick. Authoring a separate template without the attribute is the only way to do it. Somebody hit that wall and solved it correctly.

Comfort caps at +20 (ColonyMoraleDB.MaxComfortBonus = 20.0) across every building that provides it - Residency, Recreation and the Habitat - so a comfort dial that runs past 20 is inert above the cap. They ADD toward that +20, so a happy colony is built from several sources, not one - which is exactly why more than one civic building now feeds it.

The two gauges, and why it works. MORALE = are they happy → runs MigrationRate (a high-morale colony sucks population in, a low one drains out). A metropolis cranks BOTH high (jobs + amenities + policing + commerce) and snowballs as migration compounds it; a village runs the same buildings at idle and stays small. Neither gauge can be written directly — both are re-derived every month from a list of input line-items, so each dial adds ONE input to a tally the engine re-sums (the exact seam tax/food already use). An eighth cost channel - and my own rule would have flagged it wrongly

The Industrial door produced a rule: a dial is free exactly when the template's Mass formula does not read it, and proposed a test that flags any GuiSelection dial absent from all seven cost formulas. The academies are the counter-example that keeps that test honest.

Academy dialRangeIn a cost formula?But is it free?
Class Size10 -> 2,500 navalMass = Class Size x 100, crew x0.25, research x0.5no - properly priced
Class Length1 -> 48 monthsno - in none of the sevenNO - it costs TIME

NavalAcademyAtb.cs:46: graduationDate = StarSysDateTime + TimeSpan.FromDays(TrainingPeriodInMonths x 30). A 48-month program takes four years to produce its first officer where a 1-month program takes a month - so a longer course buys better graduates (the research academy's description: "a longer program graduates scientists with a higher research-competence ceiling") and pays for it in throughput. That is a real, binding cost. It is simply not one of the seven channels.

So the proposed test needs an allow-list, and writing that list is the point. A dial is honest if it is priced in any currency the player is actually constrained by - mass - volume - crew - research - credits - build points - resources - and TIME. The test should flag every dial that appears in none of the seven and require a one-line justification for each entry on the allow-list. "It costs time" is a valid entry; "nobody noticed" is not. That turns the allow-list into the design review the campaign has been doing by hand - and unlike the hand audit, it does not rot.

Where the door stands - and where the campaign does

The headline is the sharpest built-but-unreachable finding in eight doors: the morale model's employment term is a forty-point band - the widest factor in it - and it is permanently zero, in every game, because no installation in the game declares a job. The term is written, commented, two-sided, correctly denominated against workforce rather than headcount, and tested. The producer was never added. The one thing I would build out of all eight doors. Every finding across all of them is one of three shapes - Mass = a constant, Mass = a different dial, or the dial/attribute has no counterpart on the other side - and all three are mechanically detectable. One data-only test: for every player dial in every template, assert it appears in at least one of the seven cost formulas or in an AtbConstrArgs; for every *Atb class, assert it has both a producing template and a consuming reader; and keep an explicit allow-list where the honest answer is "it costs time" or "it is a choice, not a scale." It would have found every finding in this campaign, it costs no engine change and no save risk, and unlike eight pages of hand audit, it does not rot.
